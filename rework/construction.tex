\begin{section}{Motivation \& Construction Axioms}
    Initially,field theory and Galois Theory was all about solving ancient problems deemed unsolvable (in case of the quintic) so it is
    no surprise that it could be used to explain another collection of classic problems, that of compass and straightedge constructions.
    The notion of compass and straightedge constructions is usually introduced early on in math courses -- we learned the basics of construction
    including constructing paralle lines, constructing the angle bisector of an angle and the perpendicular bisector of a line segment. There are nuanced differences between 
    `construction' problems we encountered in high school and `construction' problems as accepted in field theory, so it serves us to first define 
    what a compass and straightedge are and what can they do.

    \begin{definition}{(Compass \& Straightedge)}
        A straightedge, unlike a ruler, has no measure of length. A compass is defined conventionally.
    \end{definition}

    \begin{definition}{(Operations in Compass \& Straightedge Construction)}
        In compass and straightedge construction, one can only do the three following operations:
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Draw a line through two given points;
            \item Draw a circle centered at a given point and passing through another given point (i.e. given center and radius);
            \item Obtain the point of intersection of a line and a circle or of two circles.
        \end{enumerate}
        \textbf{Note.} A common mistake here is to assume that a compass \textit{can preserve distance} -- that is, one can copy a line segment of a 
        given distance by aligning two ends of the compass to each end of the line segment. In the original (and classic) case of the construction problem
        posed by Euclid, a \textit{collapsing} compass is used, one that would collapse when you pick it up. This is for two reasons: \textbf{(a)} Euclid
        made the fewest possible assumptions about the functions of a compass and straightedge, and \textbf{(b)} it is doubtful that the ancient Greeks had 
        in their possession modern rigid compasses. \\
        Despite this, it can be proved that the two compasses are equal in construction: what one could do, the other also could. A detailed explanantion is
        included in the Proofs and Further Reading section.
    \end{definition}

    The proofs of this section aren't too difficult, so it is advised to work through them with a compass and straightedge if possible. The construction problems 
    date back thousands of years and the resolution is extremely satisfying -- have fun!
\end{section}

\begin{section}{The Four Operations, plus one}
    When delving into compass and straightedge construction, we are most concerned about which lengths can be constructed from the unit length (1). The defintion 
    of constructibility helps us formalize this.

    \begin{definition}{(Constructibility)}
        A positive real number \(m\) is \textit{constructible} if a line segment of length \(m\) can be constructed with compass and straightedge from a line segment 
        of length 1, otherwise it is not constructible.
    \end{definition}

    We might expect the compass and straightedge to be able to perform the four operations, addition, subtraction, multiplication and division. This is true, and 
    we can prove it!

    \begin{theorem}{(The Four Operations.)}
        Given constructible lengths \(a\) and \(b\), the lengths \(a+b, a-b, ab\) and \(a/b\) are all constructible.
        \begin{proof}
            We'll need the following elementary result:
            \begin{lemma}
                If \(a\) is constructible, then we can obtain on the plane the point \((a,0)\) located on the \(x\)-axis. Generally, if \(a\) is constructible, then we 
                can obtain a line segment of length \(a\) anywhere on the plane. \\
                \noindent\textbf{Note.} We might consider this statement trivial, but in Euclid's original defintion that uses a collapsing compass (non-distance preserving)
                this statement needs a rigorous proof. Details in the Proofs \& Further Reading section. In the meantime, we can assume this is true.
            \end{lemma}

            The following proofs are geometric, so assume that \(a,b\) are positive and \(a>b\) to avoid negative lengths.\\

    We can conclude that some roots are constructible. The constructible roots are none other than the square root, and we have the following:
    \begin{theorem}{(Square root)}
        If \(a\) is a positive constructible real number, then \(\sqrt{a}\) is also constructible.
        \begin{proof}
            This one is slightly trickier and uses the elementary result that the angle subtended by the diameter is \(90^{\circ}\) (and also 
            similar triangles).

    \end{theorem}

    By now we have a solid grasp of what operations are valid in compass and straightedge construction: the four operations, along with taking square 
    roots. In fact, we can show that these are the \textit{only} valid operations, with an argument of (surprisingly) analytic geometry. We can 
    formalize the `four operations \& square root' idea with field extensions:

    \begin{theorem}{(Degree of Extension from Compass \& Straightedge Construction.)}
        If the element \(\alpha \in F_k\) is obtained from field \(F\) through a series of compass and straightedge constructions, then \([F_k : F] = 2^k\)
        for some positive integer \(k\).
        \begin{proof}
            We analyze each of the 3 defined operations (see \textbf{Defintion 1.2}).\\
            \break
            \noindent\textbf{1. Finding the intersection of two lines.} A line in the plane with coordinates in \(F\) is of the form \(Ax + By + C =0\), 
            with coefficients all in \(F\). Solving for the intersection of two lines amounts to solving the simultaneous system of equations 
            \begin{align*}
                A_1 x + B_1 y + C_1 &= 0\\
                A_2 x + B_2 y + C_2 &= 0
            \end{align*}
            which has solutions also in \(F\).\\
            \break
            \noindent\textbf{2. Finding the intersection of a line and a circle.} A circle in the plane is of the form \((x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2\) with 
            \(h,k,r \in F\). Solving for the intersection of a circle and a line amounts to solving the simultaneous system of equations
            \begin{align}
                Ax + By + C &= 0\\
                (x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 &= r^2
            \end{align}
            Using the substitution \(x = -\frac{By+C}{A}\), we obtain from (2) a quadratic equation for \(y\) over \(F\). Therefore the solutions \(x,y\)
            are in an extension of degree at most 2 over \(F\).\\
            \break
            \noindent\textbf{3. Finding the intersection of two circles.} This is 
            \begin{align*}
                (x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 &= r^2\\
                (x-h')^2 + (y-k')^2 &= r'^2
            \end{align*}
            Subtracting the two equations, we obtain a linear equation in terms of \(x\) and \(y\) (the quadratic terms cancel out). Then this case is the same as 
            finding the intersection of a line and a circle, and the solutions \(x,y\) are in an extension of degree at most 2 over \(F\).\\
            \break
            We can then conclude that compass and straightedge construction gives an extension of degree \(2^k\) over a base field \(F\).
        \end{proof}
        \noindent\textbf{Note.} This leads to the notion of the field of \textit{constructible numbers}, including all real numbers in an extension of degree 
        \(2^k\) over the integers (since the base field of compass and straightedge construction is generated by 1).
    \end{theorem}

\begin{section}{The Classic Greek Construction Problems}
    Readers may have heard about (more or less) the three classic construction problems that have been troubling mathematicians since ancient times: 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item (Duplicating the Cube.) Given a cube, can we construct a cube with twice its volume?
        \item (Trisecting the Angle.) Can we construct an angle that is 1/3 of a given angle?
        \item (Squaring the Circle.) Given a circle, can we construct a square with the same area?
    \end{enumerate}
    It turns out that none of these three questions have an affirmative answer, as is often the case with longstanding suspicious unsolved problems (see: quintic,
    a big chunk of G\"odel's work, parallel postulate) and we can disprove two of the three questions easily with what we've learned today! (You can take a guess at 
    which problem is a lot harder than the other two.)

    \begin{theorem}{(Classic Construction Problems.)}
        None of the classic Greek construction problems (duplicating the cube, trisecting the angle, squaring the circle) is achievable through compass and straightedge 
        construction.
        \begin{proof}
            \noindent\textbf{1. Duplicating the Cube.} This problem is equivalent to asking whether or not \(\sqrt[3]{2}\) is constructible, since a cube with volumne 2
            would have side length \(\sqrt[3]{2}\). Since \([\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}) : \mathbb{Q}] = 3\) which is not an integer power of 2, \(\sqrt[3]{2}\) is not constructible,
            and therefore it is impossible to obtain a cube with twice the volume of a given one with only compass and straightedge construction.\\
            \break
            \noindent\textbf{2. Trisecting the Angle.} If an angle \(\theta\) is constructible, then its cosine is constructible by finding the intersection of the angle and 
            the unit circle. Our problem then becomes: given \(\cos \theta\) is \(\cos \theta/3\) constructible?\\
            We can consider a special case (this is the most widely used argument for this problem): consider \(\theta = 60^{\circ}, \cos 60^{\circ} = 1/2\). We only need to show that \(\cos 20^{\circ}\)
            is not constructible. Using the triple-angle identity for cosine we obtain:
            \[\cos \theta = 4\cos^3 \theta/3 - 3\cos \theta/3.\]
            Substituting \(\cos \theta/3 = x\) and \(\cos 60^{\circ} = 1/2\), we have 
            \[4x^3 - 3x - 1/2 = 0 \Rightarrow (2x)^3 - 3(2x) - 1=0.\]
            Let \(y = 2x\). Then \(y^3 - 3y - 1=0\), and by the Rational Root Theorem the only possible rational root of this equation is \(-1\), which is apparently not a root of 
            \(y^3 - 3y - 1\). Therefore \(y^3 - 3y - 1\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb{Q}\), and its roots are in an extension of degree 3 over \(\mathbb{Q}\). Therefore \(y\) is not 
            constructible and neither is \(\cos 20^{\circ}\).\\
            \noindent\textbf{Note.} The proof shows that it is not generally possible to trisect the angle. However, we can certainly trisect \textit{some} angles, for example \(180^{\circ}\) and \(360^{\circ}\)
            (considering the regular hexagon which has an interior angle of \(120^{\circ}\) and an exterior angle of \(60^{\circ}\), both \(60^{\circ}\) and \(120^{\circ}\) are constructible). It turns out that 
            the only angles that are constructible are integer multiples of \(3^{\circ}\), for more details see the Proofs and Further Reading section.\\
            \break
            \noindent\textbf{3. Squaring the Circle.} A circle of radius 1 has area \(\pi\), so this question is equivalent to asking whether or not \(\pi\) is constructible. The argument for classifying \(\pi\)
            as transcendental is well beyond the scope of this handout, but here we shall assume that \([\mathbb{Q(\pi)} : \mathbb{Q}]\) is infinite, so \(\pi\) is not constructible and it is impossible to construct a 
            square with the area of a given circle. The proof of the transcendentality of \(\pi\), unfortunately, is not included in the Proofs and Further Reading section because the author is also mystified. 
        \end{proof}
    \end{theorem}
\end{section}

\begin{section}{Proofs and Further Reading}
    \begin{subsection}{More Tools}
        Euclid's initial definition of the compass and straightedge included a \textit{compass} and a straightedge with no markings of distance. However, in the modern world, we encounter more frequently a \textit{rigid}
        compass that can preserve the distance between two points, and a \textit{ruler} which has markings of distance. How are the results different when we implement modern tools? First, we would 
        assume the baseline: since a rigid compass and a ruler is `better' than a collapsing compass and straightedge (at least in the conventional sense), we would expect them to be able to do anything a 
        collapsing compass and straightedge could do. This is true, at least in the case of a rigid vs. collapsing compass:
        \begin{theorem}{(Euclid's Proposition 2.)}
            It is possible, using a collapsing compass and straightedge, to construct a line segment equal to a given line segment with one end at a given point.
            \begin{proof}
                Given line segment \(AB\), we want to construct a line segment \(CF=AB\) with endpoint at \(C\).
                \begin{figure}[H]
                    \centering
                    \begin{tikzpicture}[thick, line cap=round]

                        % 1. Define base points
                        \coordinate (A) at (0,0);
                        \coordinate (B) at (1.2, 0.8);
                        \coordinate (C) at (1.5, -1.2);

                        % 2. Construct Equilateral Triangle ACD on AC
                        % This uses the calc syntax: (C) rotated 60 degrees around (A)
                        \coordinate (D) at ($(A)!1!60:(C)$);
                        
                        \draw[gray!60] (A) -- (C) -- (D) -- cycle;
                        \node[left] at (A) {$A$};
                        \node[below] at (C) {$C$};
                        \node[above] at (D) {$D$};

                        % 3. Define the Rays (Extended lines from D through A and C)
                        \path[name path=rayDA] (D) -- ($(D)!4!(A)$);
                        \path[name path=rayDC] (D) -- ($(D)!4!(C)$);
                        \draw[gray, thin] (D) -- ($(D)!3.5!(A)$);
                        \draw[gray, thin] (D) -- ($(D)!3.5!(C)$);

                        % 4. Circle at A with radius AB (to find E)
                        % We use 'name path' so we can find the intersection with the ray
                        \path[name path=circleA] (A) circle [radius={veclen(1.2,0.8)}];
                        \draw[blue] (A) -- (B);
                        \draw[blue!30] (A) circle [radius={veclen(1.2,0.8)}];

                        % 5. Find intersection E
                        \fill[red, name intersections={of=rayDA and circleA, by=E}] 
                            (E) circle (1.5pt) node[left] {$E$};

                        % 6. Circle at D with radius DE (to find F)
                        % We calculate the distance from D to E for the radius
                        \path let \p1 = ($(E)-(D)$), \n1 = {veclen(\x1,\y1)} in 
                            (D) circle (\n1) [name path=circleD];
                        \draw[orange!50, dashed] let \p1 = ($(E)-(D)$), \n1 = {veclen(\x1,\y1)} in 
                            (D) circle (\n1);

                        % 7. Find intersection F on ray DC
                        \fill[red, name intersections={of=rayDC and circleD, by=F}] 
                            (F) circle (1.5pt) node[right] {$F$};

                        % 8. Final Result: The circle at C with radius CF
                        % This radius CF will exactly equal AB
                        \draw[red, very thick] let \p1 = ($(F)-(C)$), \n1 = {veclen(\x1,\y1)} in 
                            (C) circle (\n1);
                        
                        % Labels
                        \fill (B) circle (1.5pt) node[above] {$B$};
                    \end{tikzpicture}
                \end{figure}
                \begin{enumerate}
                    \item Connect \(AC\) and construct equilateral triangle \(\triangle ACD\).
                    \item Draw a circle centered at \(A\) with radius \(AB\). Find the intersection of this circle
                    and \(AD\). Label it \(E\).
                    \item Draw a circle centered at \(D\) with radius \(DE\). Find the intersection of this circle 
                    and \(CD\). Label it \(F\). Then \(CF\) is a line segment with endpoint \(C\) and length \(CF=AB\).
                \end{enumerate}
            \end{proof}
        \end{theorem}

        Euclid's Proposition 2 gives us that a rigid compass is equivalent to a collapsing compass: a rigid compass preserves distance, 
        and a collapsing compass also could duplicate a distance (with some extra steps). What about a ruler? It turns out that a ruler is 
        slightly more powerful than the straightedge (as one would expect it to be): 
        
        \begin{theorem}{(Ruler-and-Compass Construction.)}
            If \(a\in F'\) is obtained from a base field \(F\) using a series of ruler-and-compass constructions, then \([F':F] = 2^m 3^n\)
            for some nonnegative integers \(m,n\). 
        \end{theorem}
        The ruler enables us to construct extensions of degrees 2 and 3 with its `sliding' operation. Therefore, ruler-and-compass construction 
        allows us to construct extensions of degrees 2, 3, 4 and their powers. What about degree 5? If you still remember what Galois Theory is about,
        you might have a very good answer for whether or not we can construct an extension of degree 5.
        
        \begin{corollary}
            Two of the 3 classic construction problems, \textbf{(1)} trisecting the angle and \textbf{(2)} doubling the cube, are obtainable using 
            ruler and compass construction.
        \end{corollary}
    \end{subsection}

    \begin{subsection}{Less Tools}
        Let's consider the flip side, since mathematicians always like to go looking for trouble. Compass and straightedge construction requires \textit{both}
        a compass and a straightedge. What happens if we only have a compass or a straightedge?\\
        \break
        \noindent\textbf{Compass-Only Construction.} Suppose you don't like carrying around a straightedge - a piece of infinitely long wood can be unwieldy 
        sometimes. Can you still do the construction problems as usual? 
        \begin{theorem}{(Mohr-Mascheroni Theorem.)}
            Any construction that could be done with a compass and straightedge, can be done with a compass only.
            \begin{proof}
                We only need to show that the 3 basic operations can be done with compass only (see \textbf{Definition 1.2}). For length purposes (and the 
                unfortunate discrepancy of literature on this specific subject), this handout only provides the `building blocks' of the proof: \textbf{(1)} Reflection 
                across a line, \textbf{(2)} Inversion through a circle, and \textbf{(3)} Finding the center of a circle through three points on the circle.\\
                \break
                \noindent\textbf{(1) Reflecting a point across a line.} We can recall that compass and straightedge construction allows for reflecting points across a line.
                We can do this with compass only:\\
                \noindent Suppose we want to construct the point \(C\) symmetric across the line \(AB\).
                \begin{figure}[H]
                    \centering
                    \begin{tikzpicture}[thick, scale=1.2]
                        \coordinate (A) at (0,0);
                        \coordinate (B) at (3,1);
                        \coordinate (C) at (1,2);

                        % Draw the line (faded, as we only use points A and B)
                        \draw[gray!30] ($(A)!-0.5!(B)$) -- ($(A)!1.5!(B)$);
                        \fill (A) circle (1.5pt) node[below] {$A$};
                        \fill (B) circle (1.5pt) node[below right] {$B$};
                        \fill[blue] (C) circle (2pt) node[above] {$C$};

                        % 2. Compass Step 1: Circle at A through C
                        % We use 'name path' to find intersections later
                        \path[name path=circA] (A) circle [radius={veclen(1,2)}];
                        \draw[blue!20] (A) circle [radius={veclen(1,2)}];

                        % 3. Compass Step 2: Circle at B through C
                        % Radius is distance BC: sqrt((3-1)^2 + (1-2)^2) = sqrt(5)
                        \path[name path=circB] (B) circle [radius={veclen(3-1,1-2)}];
                        \draw[green!20] (B) circle [radius={veclen(3-1,1-2)}];

                        % 4. Find the intersections of the two circles
                        % One is C, the other is the reflection C'
                        \fill [red, name intersections={of=circA and circB, by={C_orig, Cprime}}]
                            (Cprime) circle (2pt) node[below right] {$C'$};

                        % Draw visual confirmation of the reflection
                        \draw[dashed, red!50] (C) -- (Cprime);
                    \end{tikzpicture}
                \end{figure}
                Construct two circles through \(C\), one centered at \(A\) (blue) and one centered at \(B\) (green). There will be two intersections of 
                \(\odot A\) and \(\odot B\): one is \(C\), and the other is \(C'\), the reflection of \(C\) across \(AB\).\\
                \break


                \noindent\textbf{2. Inversion through a Circle.} Before we start, we must be familiar with the definition of geometric inversion:
                \begin{definition}{Inversion through a Circle.}
                    With respect to a reference circle \(\odot O\) with radius \(r\), the \textit{inversion} of point \(P\) is the point \(P'\) lying on the ray \(OP\) such that 
                    \[OP \cdot OP' = r^2.\]
                    \begin{figure}[H]
                        \centering
                        \begin{tikzpicture}[thick, scale=2]

                            % 1. Define the Center (O) and Radius (R)
                            \coordinate (O) at (0,0);

                            \coordinate (P) at (2.5, 0.5);
                            \fill[blue] (P) circle (1.5pt) node[right] {$P$};

                            
                            % Visually, we place P' at a reasonable fraction along the ray
                            \coordinate (Pprime) at ($(O)!0.3!(P)$); 
                            \fill[red] (Pprime) circle (1.5pt) node[anchor=south east] {$P'$};

                            % Draw the result
                            \draw[blue!30] (O) -- (P);
                            \fill[red] (Pprime) circle (1.5pt) node[above left] {$P'$};


                        \end{tikzpicture}
                    \end{figure}
                \end{definition}
                In a sense, inversion with respect to a circle allows us to map points in a plane onto points in a circle, with points at 
                infinite distance mapped to the center and points inside the circle to points in the exterior. In the context of the Mohr-Mascheroni
                Theorem, we are more concerned about the former -- how to map points of infinite distances on straight lines into a circle. You can 
                visualize it as pulling a tissue through a ring by the center of the tissue: points at infinite distance fold and collapse into the circle.
                Here's how we do it with compass only (with similar triangles): \\

                            \path [name intersections={of=circP and invC, by={M1, M2}}];
                            
                            \draw[blue!20] (P) circle (3.5);
                            \fill (M1) circle (1.5pt) node[above] {$M$};

                            % 4. Circle at M1 through O to find P'
                            % Radius is R=2

                            \fill[red] (Pprime) circle (1.5pt) node[below left] {$P'$};

                            % 5. Highlight Similar Triangles
                            % Triangle O-M1-P
                            \draw[blue, opacity=0.5] (O) -- (M1) -- (P) -- cycle;
                            % Triangle O-P'-M1
                            \draw[red, opacity=0.5] (O) -- (Pprime) -- (M1) -- cycle;

                    \end{tikzpicture}
                \end{figure}
                Suppose we want to find the inversion of \(P\) (in this case external to \(\odot O\)) with respect to \(\odot O\).
                \begin{enumerate}
                    \item Construct circle \(\odot P\) with radius \(OP\). There are two points of intersection of \(\odot O\) and \(\odot P\):
                    they are symmetric, so pick one of them and label it \(M_1\) The other one is \(M_2\) (not shown).
                    \item Construct circle \(\odot M_1\) with radius \(OM_1\) (i.e. radius of \(\odot O\)) and find its intersection with \(OP\). Label 
                    it \(P'\). Then \(P'\) is the inverse of the point \(P\) relative to \(\odot O\).\\
                    (If you're wondering how to locate \(P'\) when we don't have the ray \(OP\): \(OP\) is the perpendicular bisector of the line segment 
                    \(M_1 M_2\), so we can find \(P'\) by drawing two arcs centered at \(M_1\) and \(M_2\) both with radius = \(OM\).)
                \end{enumerate}
                We can show that \(P'\) is the inverse: since \(\triangle MOP\) and \(\triangle MOP'\) are both isoceles with a common bottom angle, \(\triangle MOP' \sim \triangle PMO\)
                and 
                \[\frac{OP'}{OM} = \frac{OM}{OP}.\]
                Therefore \(OP' \cdot OP = r^2\) where \(r\) is radius of \(\odot O\).\\

        \break
        \noindent\textbf{Straightedge-Only Construction.} Suppose your compass was confiscated at the TSA for being a public safety hazard. In that case, you might want this:
        \begin{theorem}{(Poncelet-Steiner Theorem.)}
            Any construction that could be done with a compass and straightedge, can be done with a straightedge only, provided that there is already a circle on the plane.
        \end{theorem}
        \noindent\textbf{Nothing -- only paper.} Suppose you left your compass and straightedge at home but still came to algebra class anyway. You pick up the paper you were supposed to 
        draw diagrams on remorsefully. Then you turn it over in your hand, and an idea comes to mind. Can you perform the classic constructions using nothing but folding 
        the paper?\\
        \break
        With origami, folding paper as a means of construction, we enter into the realm of \textit{Huzita-Hatori Axioms}, a set of axioms that dictate what can be done with paper folding just 
        as the three rules to compass-straightedge construction. The full set of axioms is given here:
        \begin{theorem}{(Huzita-Hatori Axioms.)}
            The 7 following constructions are allowed using origami:
            \begin{enumerate}
                \item Given two distinct points \(P_1\) and \(P_2\), there is a unique fold passing through both of them;
                \item Given two distinct points \(P_1\) and \(P_2\), there is a unique fold that places \(P_1\) onto \(P_2\);
                \item Given two lines \(l_1\) and \(l_2\), there is a fold that places \(l_1\) onto \(l_2\);
                \item Given a point \(P_1\) and a line \(l_1\), there is a unique fold perpendicular to \(l_1\) that passes through point \(P_1\).
                \item Given two points \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) and a line \(l_1\), there is a fold that places \(P_1\) onto \(l_1\) and passes through \(P_2\).
                \item Given two points \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) and two lines \(l_1\) and \(l_2\), there is a fold that places \(P_1\) onto \(l_1\) and \(P_2\) onto \(l_2\).
                \item Given one point \(P\) and two lines \(l_1\) and \(l_2\), there is a fold that places \(P\) onto \(l_1\) and is perpendicular to \(l_2\).
            \end{enumerate}
        \end{theorem}
        You can grab a piece of paper and try out these constructions. Even though you've probably encountered origami in kindergarten, remember that for whatever thing you think of there is a group 
        of people (usually mathematicians) who hold it to the highest interest!
    \end{subsection}
